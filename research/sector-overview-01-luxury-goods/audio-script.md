# Audio summary script | Sector Overview #1 | Luxury Goods

Let's start with the big picture. The luxury market is stabilising, not recovering. According to Bain and Altagamma, personal luxury goods, meaning leather goods, clothing, shoes, jewellery, watches, beauty and eyewear, were worth three hundred and fifty-eight billion euros in twenty twenty-five. That is down two percent on a reported basis, and flat at constant exchange rates. For twenty twenty-six, Bain's base case is growth of only two to four percent.

The third quarter of twenty twenty-six broke the recovery story. Sales at LVMH's fashion and leather goods division fell five percent organically, when analysts expected a decline of less than two percent. Gucci fell six percent. Even Hermès, the sector's benchmark, grew just four point three percent at constant currency, its slowest quarter since early twenty twenty.

Why? The first reason is China. In July twenty twenty-six, sales at the twenty-five largest luxury brands in China fell by more than ten percent year on year. A government campaign to tax wealth held offshore hit exactly the richest buyers, the ones who matter most for luxury. LVMH's sales in Asia outside Japan went from plus seven percent in the first quarter to minus five percent in the third.

The second reason is the customer base itself. Bain estimates that the number of luxury buyers fell from about four hundred million in twenty twenty-two to about three hundred and forty million in twenty twenty-five. Years of price increases pushed aspirational buyers out. In a BCG and Altagamma survey, about seventy percent of luxury consumers said they had walked away from a purchase because the price did not feel justified.

The third reason is tourism. Twenty twenty-six brought three shocks: the war in the Middle East, which disrupted the Gulf travel hubs; weaker Chinese shoppers abroad; and violent protests in France in October, with luxury stores looted in Paris, Marseille and Bordeaux.

But not everything is weak. The clear winner is hard luxury, meaning jewellery and watches. Richemont, the owner of Cartier and Van Cleef and Arpels, grew its jewellery sales by twenty-four percent at constant currency in its first fiscal quarter, its seventh double-digit quarter in a row. Jewellery is increasingly seen as a store of value, and it is less dependent on logos.

In the United States, the market has been the most resilient region, and accessible luxury is doing well. Tapestry, the owner of Coach, grew fourteen percent in its last fiscal year.

Now, the stock market. Investors have punished the sector. The MSCI Europe textiles, apparel and luxury index was down twenty-five percent year to date in early October. LVMH, Hermès and Kering were each down roughly forty percent. LVMH trades at about sixteen times expected earnings, its lowest level in more than ten years. In other words, the leaders are as cheap as they have been in a decade.

The report highlights four themes for the next one to two years. First, hard luxury should keep outgrowing fashion. Second, China is no longer the growth engine, but an option: forecasts that assume a rebound are at risk. Third, value for money is becoming the brand promise, and only true scarcity brands can still raise prices. And fourth, balance sheets will drive deals: Kering is selling assets and closing stores, while cash-rich groups like LVMH, Richemont and Hermès are natural buyers.

Finally, three names to watch, without any rating. Richemont, the cleanest exposure to jewellery, with nine point one billion euros of net cash. Hermès, whose valuation has halved in about a year, and the question is whether the market has overreacted. And Kering, the highest-risk recovery story, which depends on whether Gucci's product reset works.
