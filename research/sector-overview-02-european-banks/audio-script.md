# Audio summary script | Sector Overview #2 | European Banks

Welcome to Sector Overview, the weekly sector research series by Alexis Rondeaux. The summary is read by an AI voice. The full report, with charts, tables and sources, is on the website.

Let's start with the big picture. European banks are earning their best returns since the financial crisis. According to the European Banking Authority, EU banks posted a return on equity of eleven point three percent in the second quarter of twenty twenty-six, up from ten point seven percent a year earlier. And the leaders are far above that average. UniCredit reported a return on tangible equity of almost twenty-four percent in the first half, BBVA about twenty-two percent in the third quarter, HSBC eighteen percent and Santander almost eighteen percent over nine months.

Why are banks so profitable? The main reason is interest rates. After cutting rates from four percent to two percent between twenty twenty-four and twenty twenty-five, the European Central Bank started hiking again. It raised its deposit rate to two and a quarter percent in June twenty twenty-six, and to two and a half percent in September, pointing to inflation pressure from the war in the Middle East. Higher rates mean banks earn more on their loans than they pay on deposits. Net interest income is growing again: plus thirteen percent at Société Générale and plus sixteen and a half percent at BBVA in the third quarter.

The sector is also solid. The average core capital ratio of EU banks, the CET1 ratio, is about sixteen percent, and bad loans are below two percent of the total. That strength allows banks to return a lot of money to shareholders: about one hundred and six billion euros of dividends and buybacks in twenty twenty-five, and about one hundred and seventeen billion planned for twenty twenty-six.

The stock market has noticed. The STOXX Europe six hundred banks index rose sixty-seven percent in twenty twenty-five, its best year on record. But October twenty twenty-six was a reminder of the risks. On the twenty-eighth of October, the index was still up nine percent for the year, but down more than eight percent for the month. The trigger was France. The gap between French and German ten-year borrowing costs touched about one point eight percentage points, the widest since twenty eleven, as a minority government struggled with its budget. Because banks hold a lot of government bonds, investors started to worry again about the link between banks and their governments. French banks were hit hardest: in early October, Société Générale was about twenty-six percent below its August peak.

The other big story is consolidation. Europe's banking market is still split along national lines, but that is starting to change. On the fifteenth of October, the European Central Bank cleared UniCredit to take control of Germany's Commerzbank, where it already has an economic stake of about forty-eight percent. That would be the first large cross-border bank takeover in the euro area in years. In Italy, there is an open battle: Intesa Sanpaolo made a thirty point six billion euro offer for Monte dei Paschi, and Monte dei Paschi responded with its own offer for Banco BPM. And Santander bought the American bank Webster for twelve point two billion dollars.

How are banks valued? Not on earnings multiples like most companies, but on price to tangible book value. The median European bank traded at about one and a half times tangible book at the end of September. The logic is simple: the higher the return on equity, the higher the multiple. A bank earning about twelve percent only justifies a multiple slightly above one, so today's prices already assume that returns will improve further.

The report highlights four themes. First, higher-for-longer rates extend the profit cycle. Second, consolidation is crossing borders, if politics allows it. Third, the link between banks and their governments is back, starting with France. And fourth, regulatory relief, including a three-year delay of new market-risk rules, frees capital for buybacks and acquisitions.

Finally, three names to watch, without any rating. UniCredit, the highest-return large bank, which is about to absorb Commerzbank. Santander, which must integrate Webster while keeping its capital on target. And Société Générale, the purest play on the French sovereign question.
